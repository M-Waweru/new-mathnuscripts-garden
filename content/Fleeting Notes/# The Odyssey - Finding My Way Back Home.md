# The Odyssey - Finding My Way Back Home

## A Story That Refuses to Die

I went to the movies to watch _The Odyssey_, and I must say that it left a profound impression on me. Christopher Nolan has done it again, taking what could have remained an ancient legend and transforming it into a powerful visual narrative. The visuals, the acting, the effects, and the music were exceptional. But what impressed me most was something much deeper. He took a story that has survived for over three thousand years and made it accessible to a modern audience without stripping it of its philosophical weight. It reminded me why certain stories become timeless. They are not remembered because of their plots alone, but because they continue to reveal something true about what it means to be human.

As I left the cinema and later listened to philosophers discuss the film, I realised that the story had stirred questions that I had not expected. What keeps a man going when he is finished? What does a man do after achieving the greatest victory of his life? How does one resist the distractions that promise everything he desires but slowly rob him of who he is? These were the questions that stayed with me long after I had left the theatre. This essay is my attempt to answer them, not as a literary critic, but as someone who found himself reflected in the story.

## Victory Is Not the End

The _Odyssey_ begins where most stories would end. Odysseus has already won. After ten years of siege warfare, he secures victory for the Greeks through one of history's greatest acts of deception. Instead of defeating Troy by brute force, he convinces the Trojans to bring in a giant wooden horse into their city, believing it to be a peace offering. Hidden inside are Greek soldiers who emerge during the night, open the city gates, and allow the Greek army to enter and destroy Troy. It is a brilliant military strategy, yet it is also an act that breaks the sacred laws of Zeus. Victory comes, but it comes at a terrible moral cost.

After the war, the Greek armies begin sailing home. Odysseus, however, decides to take a different route. Believing he can return faster while plundering villages allied to Troy, he leads his men away from the known path and into the unknown. It is this decision, driven partly by confidence and partly by greed, that begins his true Odyssey. The further they travel, the more lost they become. The winds carry them into places no man intended to reach.

Their journey becomes a succession of trials. They are trapped by the Cyclops and survive only through wit rather than strength. They lose most of their fleet to an island of giant warriors. They encounter a goddess who transforms men into pigs by appealing to their appetites. Every island presents another temptation, another disaster, another lesson. Looking back, I realised that these encounters were never simply monsters or magical creatures. They were symbols of the internal struggles that every person eventually faces.

## My Own Odyssey

That is where the story stopped being about Odysseus and became about me.

In a very real sense, I am on my own Odyssey. I am wandering, trying to find my way back home to myself. My body is physically at home. I am surrounded by my family, my friends, my work, and the business I am trying to build. Yet there remains a persistent feeling that I have not truly arrived. I long for a place I have never been, or perhaps a place I once knew but somehow lost.

For me, home has always existed somewhere in the future. Home is building Thenks into a world-class company worth billions. Home is writing a masterpiece of essays in the Mathnuscripts. Home is reaching the point where money is no longer a source of anxiety. I have convinced myself that once I achieve these things, I will finally have permission to become the person I truly want to be. Only then will I travel more freely, write more honestly, cultivate deeper friendships, pursue forgotten passions, and enjoy life without guilt.

But as I reflected on Odysseus, I began to question whether I have become attached to the journey itself.

I say that I want to go home, but do I really?

What am I waiting for?

What am I desperately craving that I believe this Odyssey will finally give me?

Perhaps my greatest fear is not failure.

Perhaps it is arriving.

Throughout the story, Odysseus repeatedly chooses paths that prolong his own suffering. Instead of sailing directly home, he pursues more wealth, more conquest, and more opportunities. Every detour takes him further away from the very thing he claims to desire most. I recognised something painfully familiar in that pattern. How often have I told myself that I only need one more milestone before I can finally rest? One more product launch. One more investment. One more achievement. One more essay. The destination keeps moving because I keep moving it.

The _Odyssey_ suggests something unsettling. Sometimes we are not lost because we cannot find home. Sometimes we are lost because we are afraid of what home requires. Home demands stillness. It demands contentment. It demands confronting ourselves without the distraction of another mountain to climb.

## The Crew We Carry

Another lesson that struck me deeply was leadership.

Odysseus never travels alone.

Every decision he makes affects the lives of his crew. Sometimes his wisdom saves them. Sometimes his mistakes cost them dearly. Sometimes they refuse to trust him, placing themselves and everyone else in danger.

Leadership is not simply about making the right decisions.

It is about carrying responsibility for people whose lives are shaped by your choices.

As I reflected on my own work, I realised that founders experience something similar. Every strategic decision affects employees, customers, investors, and families. We often imagine leadership as influence or authority, but more often it feels like responsibility.

There are moments when every available option leads to loss. One path may save everyone but risks losing everything. Another guarantees that some will perish while ensuring the survival of the rest. These are impossible decisions, and often they cannot be fully explained beforehand. The people you are trying to protect may even come to resent you. Yet leadership sometimes demands standing firm despite being misunderstood.

## Appetite, Temptation, and Forgetting Who You Are

The greatest dangers in the story are not always violent.

The Sirens promise everything a man desperately wants to hear. They whisper of missed opportunities, forgotten dreams, and alternate lives that could have been lived. They tempt us with fantasies rather than truth.

The Lotus Eaters and the nymphs offer something even more dangerous: comfort.

They invite a person to forget who they are, to abandon their purpose, and to remain suspended in pleasure where nothing changes and nothing truly matters. They distract you with sex, with stimulation, with comfort, with all the things that promise happiness while quietly taking years from your life.

Odysseus spends seven years trapped in such comfort. What fascinated me was not simply that he stayed so long. It was that even during those years, something inside him refused to disappear. Beneath the pleasure remained a quiet restlessness. There was always a feeling that there was somewhere else he needed to be. There was still a life, a mission, and a purpose waiting for him.

I understand that feeling.

There are seasons when distraction, addiction, comfort, or ambition can make us forget ourselves. Yet somewhere beneath all of it remains a small voice reminding us that this is not who we really are.

The only way Odysseus escaped was by remembering.

He confronted the failures, the pain, the trauma, the triumph, and the person he had once been. Only by remembering could he find the strength, the will, and, most importantly, the reason to continue the journey home.

## The Limits of Control

For years, Odysseus survives because of intelligence, strategy, and determination. He constantly tries to outwit fate itself. Much of the time, that brilliance saves him.

Yet eventually he reaches a point where effort alone is no longer enough.

He builds a simple raft, lets go, and entrusts himself to the sea.

He surrenders.

Not because he has become weak, but because he finally understood that control has limits.

That may be the hardest lesson for ambitious people. We believe every problem can be solved by working harder, thinking smarter, and refusing to give up. Often that mindset serves us well.

But not always.

There comes a point where we must stop trying to force reality into our plans and instead allow ourselves to be carried by something greater than ourselves. Whether one calls it faith, grace, providence, or simply acceptance, there is wisdom in recognising that not everything belongs within our control.

## Can You Be Home and Still Be Lost?

When Odysseus finally reaches Ithaca, his story still does not end. Unlike Agamemnon, who returned triumphantly only to be murdered, Odysseus enters quietly, disguised as a beggar. He observes before acting. He reclaims his home not through celebration but through patience and discernment.

Yet even after reclaiming his kingdom, reuniting with Penelope, and restoring order, he cannot fully settle.

He eventually leaves again.

His son can remain.

Odysseus cannot.

He is still running from something inside himself.

That disturbed me because it revealed a tragic possibility.

You can spend your entire life trying to get home, only to discover that you have forgotten how to live there.

The greatest obstacle was never the sea.

It was the man himself.

## The Weight of Victory

What struck me most was that after Troy, one would expect Odysseus to feel like a hero.

Instead, he carries himself like a man haunted by guilt.

He won through deception. He participated in the destruction of an entire city. The victory that made him legendary also became the source of his deepest burden. It is only during his long journey home that the weight of his actions finally catches up with him. He wants to atone, yet he no longer knows how. Some wounds cannot be undone simply by saying sorry. Some consequences become permanent companions.

I found that fascinating because it raises a question that every ambitious person eventually has to face.

What happens after the victory?

What if the thing you spent your entire life pursuing leaves you with guilt instead of satisfaction?

What if success itself becomes another burden that you must carry?

## The Real Odyssey

There is so much to learn from this story, but above all else, I see myself in Odysseus. I recognise the impossible decisions, the ambition, the distractions, the victories that come with unexpected costs, and the desire to atone for mistakes that cannot simply be erased. He is not the same man who left Ithaca for Troy, nor the same man who sailed away after victory. He has become someone entirely different. Yet even he struggles to understand who that new person is.

Like many men, I crave my own Iliad. I crave my own Odyssey. I want a life worthy of being remembered. But this story reminds me that every meaningful life comes with a cost. Great stories are not built only on triumphs. They are built on suffering, responsibility, temptation, sacrifice, failure, and the difficult work of becoming someone different.

The _Odyssey_ is both a blueprint and a warning. It teaches us what to pursue, but perhaps more importantly, what to avoid. It reminds us that the greatest dangers are rarely the monsters outside us. They are the attitudes, appetites, ambitions, pride, and unresolved wounds within us that slowly pull us off course.

I have often wished for an experience that would strip away all the clutter inside me and permanently change my perspective. Perhaps it is seeing Earth from space. Perhaps it is surviving tragedy. Perhaps it is confronting loss so profound that everything I currently value is reordered overnight. Yet I also know I do not truly wish for those things. I fear what they would make of me. I fear the responsibilities they would place upon me. I fear the irreversible choices they would force me to make.

Perhaps wisdom does not require catastrophe.

Perhaps it requires only remembering.

Remembering who we truly are.

Remembering where we are trying to go.

Remembering that home is not merely a destination but a way of being.

All we can do is hope, endure, take responsibility, learn from our failures, remember who we are, and continue finding our way back whenever we become lost.

It may take years.

But what else is there to do?

Either we find our way home...

Or we remain lost at sea.

Like Odysseus, I am still somewhere lost at sea.

And I am trying to find my way back home.
